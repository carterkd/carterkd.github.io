Publications

The Elasticity of Quantitative Investment

Review of Financial Studies · Lead article and editor’s choice

Presentations: University of Utah, University of Washington, Yale, Purdue, London Business School, Boston University, Brigham Young University Economics, Brigham Young University Finance, and Indiana University.

Published paper ↗
Abstract

What is the demand elasticity of statistical arbitrageurs that invest according to the advice of modern cross-sectional asset pricing models? Thirteen models from the literature exhibit strikingly inelastic demand, in contrast to classical models that rely on statistical arbitrageurs to create elastic market demand for assets. This inelasticity arises from the difficulty of trading against price changes. A quantitative equilibrium model shows that aggregate demand remains inelastic even with these statistical arbitrageurs in the market.

Why Do Portfolio Choice Models Predict Inelastic Demand?

With Mahyar Kargar and Jiacui Li

Journal of Financial Economics

Presentations: NBER LTAM, AFA, MFA, Maryland Young Scholars, UConn Finance Conference, and Wabash Finance.

Published paper ↗ Working-paper page ↗
Abstract

Classical asset pricing models predict that optimizing investors exhibit extremely high demand elasticities, while empirical estimates are significantly lower—by three orders of magnitude. To reconcile this disparity, we introduce a novel decomposition of investor demand elasticity into two key components: “price pass-through,” which captures how price movements forecast returns, and “unspanned returns,” reflecting a stock’s lack of perfect substitutes. In a factor model framework, we show that unspanned returns become significant when models include “weak factors.” Classical models overestimate demand elasticity by assuming both very low unspanned returns and high price pass-throughs, assumptions that are inconsistent with empirical evidence.

Public working papers

The Limits of Factor Model Spanning

With Fahiz Baba-Yara and Brian Boyer

Revise and resubmit at the Review of Financial Studies

Presentations: Wharton Micro Finance Seminar, Craig Holden Memorial Conference, Red Rock Finance Conference, Indiana University, and Brigham Young University.

SSRN ↗
Abstract

Factor models, their linear stochastic discount factors, and the mean–variance-efficient portfolios formed from their factors are central tools for describing expected returns. We ask whether any factor model proposed in the literature can price the model portfolios produced by all the others. We soundly reject this null: every candidate produces economically large pricing errors—spanning alphas—on portfolios produced by other models. Covariance-based SDF tests likewise cannot price every candidate, even when its SDF coefficient is chosen solely to price competing model portfolios. Even meta-portfolios constructed from all candidate models fail to span them out of sample. We develop a theory explaining how rational procedures trained on the same data can continue to produce different pricing portfolios in a high-dimensional learning problem.

The Origins of the Factor Zoo: Investors Weakly Substitute Across Stocks

With Aditya Chaudhry

Presentations: 2026 Notre Dame Investment Management Conference, EFA (2026), UBC Summer Finance Conference 2026, 2026 Princeton Conference on Asset Demand Systems, and The Ohio State University.

SSRN ↗
Abstract

We propose a simple explanation for the “factor zoo” in expected stock returns: investors treat individual stocks as weak substitutes. In classical asset-pricing models, substitution is strong: investors readily reallocate across assets that comove, so differences in expected returns are explained by the few factors that create the most comovement across stocks. In contrast, we show that substitution is empirically weak by measuring cross-sectional return predictability, cross-price multipliers, and cross-price elasticities. This weak substitution gives rise to the factor zoo: expected returns depend on many factors, including weak factors that create little comovement. Moreover, weak substitution rationalizes why equilibrium Sharpe ratios remain moderate even though weak factors contribute to expected returns. Additionally, weak substitution implies that misspecified substitution patterns create only small biases in price elasticity and multiplier estimates.

On the Recovery of Demand Elasticities in Dynamic Settings

With Mahyar Kargar, Jiacui Li, and Dejanir Silva

Presentations: NBER New Developments in Long-Term Asset Management Spring 2026 Meeting, SFS Cavalcade 2026, WFA 2026, Boston College, Carnegie Mellon University, The Ohio State University, Duke Asset Pricing, and UCLA.

SSRN ↗
Abstract

We study how to recover and use demand elasticities in dynamic asset markets. In demand-system asset pricing, a price movement is accompanied by changes in future expected prices, returns, and risk, so there is no single context-free elasticity. The instrumented elasticity, which captures the reduced-form response to a shock that moves prices together with these future objects, is shock-specific. The structural elasticity, which holds the other arguments of demand fixed, is transportable across shock environments. We show that the inverse of the instrumented elasticity is the price multiplier and develop a recovery theory that uses impulse responses of flows, returns, and risk to extract structural demand coefficients from shock-specific reduced-form estimates. We then implement the recovery in an orthonormal factor model that accommodates unbalanced stock panels. In weekly order-flow data, the naive inverse-multiplier benchmark is about 0.35, while the recovered stock-specific own-price elasticity is about 5. A four-week sign-restricted recovery finds precisely estimated expected-return sensitivity in systematic factor demand at every week, even though the stock-specific component is concentrated in week one. Risk raises the first-week elasticity and displays suggestive continuation dynamics.

Complexity Breaks Arbitrage Pricing Theory

With Alejandro Lopez-Lira

Fisher College of Business Working Paper No. 2026-03-005 · Charles A. Dice Center Working Paper No. 2026-05

Presentations: Chicago Booth Asset Pricing Conference, Carnegie Mellon University, University of Florida, CUNEF University, ITAM, Seoul National University, Nova School of Business and Economics, TBEAR Asset Pricing Workshop, European Finance Association, Michigan State University, Korea University, and Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST).

SSRN ↗
Abstract

Arbitrage Pricing Theory predicts pricing errors vanish as markets grow. Three findings reverse this prediction. First, under estimation uncertainty, equilibrium pricing errors scale as N/t (number of assets over sample size), with a persistent component from time-varying fundamentals that no estimator can eliminate. Second, the stochastic discount factor loads primarily on estimation errors rather than fundamental risk factors, explaining why risk-based factor models fail to price assets. Third, multiple strategies achieve high Sharpe ratios with low cross-correlations as they exploit distinct dimensions of the growing estimation error space. When investors disagree about expected returns, mispricing persists even with informed investors because risk aversion prevents them from taking sufficiently large positions to eliminate complexity-driven pricing errors. Empirically, individual stock returns require 145 principal components to explain half their variance. Systematic hedge funds’ active positions exhibit near-zero correlations (average 0.07), consistent with exploitation of distinct estimation-error dimensions.

Do Household Biases Matter for Asset Prices?

With Samuli Knüpfer, Jens Soerlie Kvaerner, Bahar Sen-Dogan, and Petra Vokata

Presentations: European Finance Association, BI Norwegian Business School, CUNEF University, Nordic Finance Association, CEPR Household Finance Conference, Leibniz University Hannover, Midwest Finance Association, Norwegian School of Economics, Oslo Metropolitan University, and Tilburg University.

SSRN ↗
Abstract

Are household behavioral biases quantitatively important for asset prices? We answer this question using complete ownership records for Norwegian stocks from 2007 to 2020 and a demand-system model of asset prices. Households account for 1.8 times their market share of cross-sectional return variation. Wealthy investors with strong behavioral tilts drive most of this effect. For overconfidence, lottery preference, and the disposition effect, reallocating capital from the most- to the least-biased households compresses anomaly spreads on average. Return-extrapolating demand instead absorbs the average anomaly spread, with book-to-market as the primary creator-side exception. Our results show that measured household behavioral demand matters in the aggregate, but its effect on anomaly pricing depends on the measure.

Erasing Alpha

With Xiao Han, Stanislav Sokolinski, and Andrea Tamoni

Presentations: 2025 Conference on New Perspectives and New Products in Asset Management at Bayes Business School, 2025 SAFE Asset Pricing Conference, 2025 CFEA Conference, 13th HEC–McGill Winter Finance Workshop, FSU Truist Beach Conference (2026), FIRS Conference (2026), 11th University of Connecticut Finance Conference, Third UIC Finance Conference, NFA Annual Conference (2026), and AFA Annual Conference (2027).

SSRN ↗
Abstract

How much capital must move to eliminate the alpha of an investment strategy? We address this question within a stock-level equilibrium framework that delivers a transparent decomposition of alpha erosion into three components: price impact, its pass-through into expected returns, and the ownership overlap between the targeted portfolio and the source and destination of capital. Focusing on the value strategy, we find that alpha is remarkably hard to erase: even reallocating the entire growth-fund AUM into value funds reduces value alpha from 5.6% to only 4.9%. This modest erosion implies a value capacity of about $3.1 trillion, and alternative funding sources yield even larger capacity. The same reallocation generates a whack-a-mole spillover by raising momentum alpha. Our results suggest that even capital flows large enough to generate meaningful price impact translate into only modest changes in alphas, and that overlapping portfolio holdings can mechanically redistribute alpha across strategies rather than eliminate it.

Is Asset Demand Elasticity Set at the Household or Intermediary Level?

With Ehsan Azarmsa

Presentations: SFS Cavalcade, Yiran Fan Memorial Conference, and AFA Annual Meeting.

SSRN ↗
Abstract

Household-based and intermediary-based asset pricing models disagree about the elasticity of the allocations to intermediaries. Household-based models imply a high elasticity that renders intermediaries’ portfolio behavior irrelevant, while intermediary-based models imply a low elasticity. Using 13F institutional holdings data, we find that allocations to mutual funds and investment advisors exhibit a negative and significant relationship with the price of their portfolio assets. However, this elasticity is too small to significantly impact aggregate demand elasticity for individual assets. Our findings support the central premise of intermediary-based models: households do not adjust allocations enough to counteract mispricings caused by intermediary frictions.

Good Variance, Bad Variance: Cash-Flows, Discount Rates, and the Risk-Return Relationship

With Brandon Bates, Brian Boyer, and Tyler Shumway

Presentations: SFS Cavalcade, Wasatch Conference, and Brigham Young University.

Paper ↗
Abstract

We decompose conditional variance into the sum of two components: one driven by uncertainty about cash-flow growth, or bad variance, and a second driven by uncertainty about discount rates, or good variance. We develop a simple theoretical model with time-varying second conditional moments and document empirical evidence that suggests bad variance earns a risk premium that is statistically and economically significant, whereas good variance does not. In out-of-sample tests, bad variance dominates other predictors of market returns and can help identify the gap between the lower bound of Martin (2016) and the actual market risk premium.

Profit Puzzles and the Fall of Public-Firm Profit Rates

With Alexandre Sollaci and James Traina

Presentations: Indiana University, Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco, International Monetary Fund, and NBER Summer Institute Macroeconomics and Productivity.

Paper ↗
Abstract

Why have U.S. aggregate profit rates increased while financial-market rates decreased since 1980? We propose a mismatch hypothesis: profit rates in the national accounts track the return on capital for all firms, while financial-market rates track the cost of capital for public firms only. We show public-firm profit rates halved since 1980, matching trends in financial markets and suggesting low market power. Nonfinancial domestic private-firm profit rates doubled, suggesting high market power or risk. Size and sector differences cannot explain the divergence, though intangible intensity might. Our results indicate substantial biases in extrapolating public-firm trends to the aggregate economy.

Classical Versus Demand System Asset Pricing

Presentations: Eastern Finance Association Annual Meeting.

Paper ↗
Abstract

Classical asset pricing (CAP) models often assume that asset demand is linear in log prices, while demand system asset pricing (DSAP) relies on near-isoelastic demand. Although isoelastic demand arises from a particular coefficient restriction—and exotic alternatives like cosine demand are possible—it fits the data better than a CAP model. CAP models predict similar percentage price impacts across stocks of very different sizes, whereas DSAP predicts smaller impacts for larger stocks, consistent with observed demand curves. I show that DSAP demand better matches the data by measuring price multipliers across the size distribution.

Mispricing and the Elasticity of Quantitative Investment

Abstract

What happens to mispricing when statistical arbitrageurs, investing with prominent factor models from the literature, enter the market? Counterfactual experiments using an asset-demand-system methodology show that mispricing increases or only marginally decreases when these models are used to manage capital. This indicates that it is either quite difficult to eliminate mispricing or that modern factor models have serious empirical shortfalls.

Investment Mandates and Diversification

Predictable Downturns

Inactive

Paper ↗
Abstract

Eugene Fama stated in his Nobel Prize lecture that “there is no statistically reliable evidence that expected stock returns are sometimes negative” (2013). However, various theoretical models imply that expected stock returns are sometimes negative. This paper provides evidence that expected excess aggregate stock-market returns are sometimes negative, and that portfolios composed of the most liquid stocks have predictable downturns as well. A forecasting model relying exclusively on ex-ante information predicts stock-market downturns when the day-prior confidence of a downturn is relatively high. The average excess return on predicted downturn days is −13.9 basis points. A market-timing portfolio using these predictions generates a risk-adjusted return of 3.5 basis points per day, annualized to an average 8.8% risk-adjusted return.
